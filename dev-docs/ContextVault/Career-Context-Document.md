# Career Context Document — Eric Rash

*A reference profile for career advice, strategic thinking, and work-problem support. Built from a structured interview on June 8, 2026. Meant to be read by future-Claude (or Eric) before giving career-related guidance, so advice is grounded in Eric's actual situation rather than generic best practice.*

## Snapshot

Eric is the **Director of Applied Performance at Baylor University Athletics**, roughly eight years into his time in the department, having been promoted up into this role from within. The role carries a director title and broad autonomy over *what* he works on, but almost no budget, no clear mandate, a supervisor who sets few expectations, and a reporting change that cost him access to senior leadership. He is privately on a self-imposed ~12-month clock (set earlier in 2026): build skills, invest in people, create frameworks, then reassess whether to stay or leave — and he does not expect his long-term future to be at Baylor. His honest read is that the role as currently defined does not create enough value and needs to be redefined, and that shifting economics in college athletics may eventually eliminate the role regardless. His real ambition points toward an analytics-driven performance role where he is both a decision-maker and still hands-on, ideally back home near family in Iowa.

The throughline: a genuinely strong diagnostician and builder stuck in an environment with no accountability structure to carry his work into execution, in a seat that lost its visibility.

## 1. Current Role & Responsibilities

**Title:** Director of Applied Performance, Baylor University Athletics. Applied Performance / sport science sits *behind* the athlete-facing units (athletic medicine, athletics performance/strength & conditioning, mental health, nutrition) as a support-and-enablement function rather than a direct athlete-facing one. Three primary focus areas: (1) education, research & development; (2) analytics; (3) planning.

**Reports to:** Chris Ruf (Director of Athletics Performance, i.e. Strength & Conditioning) — a same-level "director" who became Eric's supervisor after a reporting change. Chris values good communication, follow-through, "keeping things in order," and "executing at a high level," but gives little direction and has never articulated a vision. Eric cannot name how his performance is actually measured month to month — the expectations are genuinely unclear. Chris also does Eric's performance reviews. Eric finds him unapproachable.

**Day-to-day:** Attending whatever meetings are on the calendar; walking the building to check in with strength coaches, athletic medicine, and administrators to find out what they need; attending some training sessions (football is the high utilizer of his area; basketball is self-contained and resistant to involvement); and desk work on data projects, strategic planning, and OKR initiatives. A large share of his time is unstructured relationship/problem-finding work — figuring out what problems people have and how he can help.

**Decision rights:**
- *Owns outright:* what projects to pursue, what questions to chase, how to source data (public or internal). Can take a meeting with almost anyone on campus.
- *Needs sign-off (Chris):* any spending/equipment (the department has **no budget**, so this rarely happens), hiring. To get anything approved he has to build a compelling case aligned to Chris's vision — made nearly impossible because that vision has never been articulated.
- *No real control:* whether his ideas get executed (depends entirely on others), and whether other units engage with him (depends on his persistence and their openness).

**Who relies on him:** Honestly, no one is *dependent* on him. He works to make himself useful — small projects with individual coaches who found value, plus the football program's full-time tech/data coordinator handles day-to-day drop-ins. By his own assessment, "anybody can do this job; nothing breaks if I'm out for two weeks." This lack of indispensability is a core problem he's aware of.

**Accountability:** He defines his own OKRs; no one holds him to them. An annual report is due end of **July 2026** that must speak to these areas.

**The pivotal event:** Eric was previously on **administrative senior staff, reporting to Kenny.** Last year (≈2025) the reporting line changed and he was moved under Chris Ruf. Kenny framed it as "too many direct reports," not a demotion — but it felt like one, and Eric said so at the time. The real cost: he **lost access to Kenny and to higher-level conversations.** Texts go unreturned; he doesn't feel he can drop by. He's been relegated to a "tertiary player" — left off emails, out of meetings, input not solicited. He doesn't actually know the cause (reorg vs. personal vs. devaluation).

**His own definition of a successful week:** finishing his task list; moving OKRs forward; interfacing with other sport programs; being **included** in projects and higher-level work. The deepest version: being brought into higher-level decisions again, the way he was on senior staff.

## 2. Skills & Strengths

**Core strength — diagnosis and framing.** Eric is genuinely strong at unpacking a problem, facilitating a root-cause conversation, and identifying both the real problem and candidate solutions *given enough context*. His instinct is to ask better, deeper questions rather than the field-default move of "collect more data and see what it says."

- *Proof point (return-to-play):* Most practitioners drop an athlete on the force plates and watch metrics trend, then clear them when an asymmetry crosses an arbitrary threshold (e.g., 20% → 19%, "under threshold, good to go"). Eric reframes the actual question: which metrics matter for *this* athlete and sport (eccentric vs. concentric, contraction time, impulse/phase metrics), and whether **pre-injury baseline performance has been restored or exceeded** — not whether an arbitrary asymmetry line was crossed. That is a level of diagnostic rigor most peers skip.

**The "unofficial IT / tech person."** People come to him to troubleshoot or interface with technology and data — programming questions, injury-related questions, data visualization. He's become the de facto tech go-to.

**AI fluency — ahead of his peers.** He uses AI heavily and effectively for data analysis, dashboard creation, report generation, and synthesizing meeting notes. The result: he works faster, goes deeper, and turns around richer insight than colleagues. He half-buries this ("too far ahead for people to appreciate"), but it's a real, current capability differentiator.

**Systems thinking / process discipline.** His philosophy before any data problem: Is the infrastructure in place? Is the right question being asked? Have we identified what data is needed to answer it? — *before* collecting and analyzing. This sequencing is not the prevailing approach in his space.

**Where he's weaker (his own assessment):** the **execution and resource-gathering layers** — turning identified solutions into shipped, adopted reality. This depends heavily on coalition-building and on other people executing, both of which are hard in his environment. He's actively working on coalition-building.

**Underutilized:** his diagnostic/root-cause mind and his AI fluency are barely leveraged in the role — "no one comes to me for that specific thing." He doesn't get pulled into the high-level problem-unpacking conversations where he'd be most valuable.

## 3. Challenges & Friction

**Hardest part:** chasing people down and trying to *persuade* them that asking harder questions and adopting more systematic processes is worth it. There's a constant element of begging colleagues to be more organized, with no authority to require it.

**Structural / cultural problems (org-wide):**
- **No budget**, which caps what the function can do.
- **No accountability and no standards.** No one mandates what gets done; people "run off to their corners" and are left alone. No repercussions, no level-set, no shared standard. Different sports and different people are treated differently — endless one-offs and concessions that breed inconsistency across departments.
- **No shared vision to rally around** — "everybody for themselves." There's a cultural undertone of "if you're unhappy, that's your problem; go figure it out / just work harder."
- **Lost leadership access** (the reporting change) — arguably the change that hurt most.

**Where he's plateaued:** visibility, scope, and authority. He has no real mandate ("do this if you like") and isn't seen as an authority on anything.

**Self-acknowledged contribution to the problem:** He was promoted up from within, so the role was never treated with fresh seriousness — it was "business as usual." At the time he lacked the confidence to be assertive and stake out authority, and that window has largely closed and solidified. He also admits he has **not done enough to educate colleagues on what he does, what's possible, and what he can take off their plates** — a marketing/visibility gap he can still act on.

**Politics:** People in certain positions need to be seen as valuable (self-preservation), so they close him out; there's little recourse. Basketball is self-contained. Chris and Kenny are both hard to reach.

**What he dreads:** ideation and vision-casting — not because he dislikes the thinking (it's his strength) but because he knows it relies on others to execute and will likely **die in the execution gap** for lack of administrative accountability. Watching his best thinking go nowhere, year after year on the same issues, is the most frustrating part.

## 4. Energy & Motivation

**Energizes him / loses track of time:** building and creating (dashboards, reports, narratives from data, slide decks), running analysis, mining insights, being at training sessions/practice, and interacting with student-athletes. Creating, building, and sharing time with the people around him.

**Depletes him (even though competent):** repetitive meetings on the same topics that get little attention and never move. The Groundhog-Day quality of the work.

**Feels most valuable when:** someone brings a genuine question and they solve it together; he's asked to take on more or his opinion is sought; he can speak into a larger initiative; or someone *outside his department* seeks his advice / his name comes up as someone who can help. (Note: being sought out is a strong motivator for him — see the Personal Constitution tension between service and the desire to be seen.)

**Sunday test (what he'd happily dig into):** analysis and report/outline construction, prepping a presentation, a call with a colleague, and especially mining insights from accumulated data and thinking about training prescription.

**Key tension:** the role currently delivers a lot of the circular meetings that drain him and little of the build-that-gets-used work that fuels him.

**Constraints:**

**Underused leverage (the crux):** Because the perception problem is fundamentally an **output problem**, his highest-leverage move is shipping visible proof, not more consensus-building. He has the data, tools, AI speed, and autonomy to build things without asking permission. Concrete shippable outputs he himself named, mostly tied to existing OKRs:
- Dashboards and reports.
- Insight-mining from existing data sources, plus formulating and answering sharp questions.
- The **Education Initiative** — "Performance Academy" / "Performance School" content build-out.
- A newsletter, PowerPoint/teaching decks, and a social media campaign aligned to OKRs.

## 6. Growth & Ambition

**Likely not at Baylor long-term.** He expects the environment to push him out before he's fully ready, unless a genuinely better situation appears. He's "trudging through the same muck."

**Direction he wants:** more **analytics**-driven performance work. Honest gap: he doesn't yet have a traditional analytics skill set — he leans almost entirely on AI tools and lacks the foundational understanding those roles assume. He also lacks a network to support a pivot (to pro sport, industry, or another collegiate program).

**The role he wants (clarified):** a **player-coach with the wheel** — the decision-maker who sets tone, direction, and which levers to pull, *while still getting his hands dirty* generating insights and communicating them to coaching staff. Not purely IC analyst, not purely detached executive — both, with authority being the bigger driver.

**Dream geography:** back near **Iowa**, where all his close family lives (mom, mother-in-law, sister & brother-in-law, niece), where he and his wife went to school, and where he has a strong network of lifelong friends. University of Iowa would be ideal — no role currently exists there. **Comp floor: low/mid-$90Ks** to maintain current commitments.

**Timeline:** no hard external pressure beyond his **self-imposed ~12-month window** (reassess stay/leave), or sooner if Baylor moves to push him out.

**Skills to develop:**
- Foundational **data-analytics / data-analyst craft** (beyond AI-assisted output) — data manipulation techniques, systems of analytical thinking.
- Continued mastery of AI tooling.
- **Network expansion** and being viewed as a serious player in the space.
- **Coalition-building, claiming authority, and execution** (carried over from his named weaknesses).

**If he couldn't fail:** he'd take far more charge — stay on the table for more, push larger initiatives, assume control of multiple areas, force clear objectives and key results, and demand accountability and clear expectations. His hesitation is partly about whether that assertiveness would turn people off, but his conviction is that the place needs exactly that level of assertion and standard-setting.

## 7. The Real Talk

**What he wants but rarely says:** to **matter** — to be a high-credibility, highly-regarded, deeply knowledgeable voice who has contributed to the body of work in his industry and whom others lean on for advice. Not prestige for its own sake; credibility and contribution. He wants to develop other practitioners and be a **servant leader**, believing that if he takes care of his craft and his people, conventional success follows.

**Magic wand:** to be treated, from day one of this role, as a credible authority — sought after rather than chasing people. (Tied to the regret that he entered the role from within without claiming authority while the window was open.)

**What he's tolerating that he shouldn't:** a culture where people don't ask hard questions, don't think deeply, and hide behind "I'm too busy" to avoid the extra work and harder thinking.

**Honest definition of career success:** being part of a **fully aligned organization** of people who challenge each other, genuinely want to be the best at their craft, are invested in each other personally and professionally, care about each other deeply, and aren't ruled by money or others' perceptions. Income is a floor (support his family, access to what they need for their goals), not the point. Held inside his faith: that he stays grounded, takes nothing for granted, and recognizes all success as part of the divine plan of his Creator, Jesus Christ.

## How to Use This Document

When advising Eric on career matters, weight these realities:

1. **He's a diagnostician and builder trapped in an execution-poor, accountability-free environment.** Advice that assumes he can mandate adoption will fail. The realistic levers are *visible output he controls* and *coalition-building*, not authority he doesn't have.
2. **His leverage is shipping proof, not winning arguments.** The fastest path to changing the "they don't do much" perception is concrete, repeated, visible deliverables tied to OKRs (dashboards, education content, newsletter, decks) — things he can build solo with AI without permission.
3. **He's on a ~12-month decision clock** and his honest center of gravity is *leaving* Baylor for an analytics-forward, decision-making-but-hands-on role, ideally near Iowa, at ≥ low-$90Ks. Skill-building (real analytics fundamentals) and network-building are the two gating constraints for that move — treat them as urgent, not someday.
4. **Recognition and "mattering" are real motivators for him,** and there's a known tension (from his Personal Constitution work) between genuine service and the desire to be seen. Good advice helps him build credibility through contribution without letting validation-seeking drive the decisions.
5. **Faith is his organizing frame.** Career strategy that ignores his commitment to servant leadership, alignment, and grounded non-attachment to outcomes will not resonate.
6. **He asks to be challenged.** He wants Socratic pressure on vague or inconsistent reasoning, not affirmation — and he revises toward more accurate framing when pushed.
