# Athletic training utilization metrics
## Standard definitions for internal benchmarking

Draft v2, June 2026. For review and approval.

---

### Why this document exists

"Utilization" means different things to different people right now. One trainer counts the athletes they saw. Another counts being at practice. Until we count the same things the same way, no comparison between teams is fair, and nothing we send up to leadership will hold up to a second question.

This page defines the five metrics we will use in all utilization reporting, the assumptions behind them, and how we decide whether a team's data is complete enough to compare. These definitions also feed two other things: the annual per-team reports and the minimum documentation expectations.

---

### What utilization is, and what it is not

Utilization is active athlete care: a documented encounter where a trainer evaluated or treated an athlete.

It is not availability. Being onsite, at practice, or on call matters, and it is part of the job. But presence is not care. Availability and utilization will be reported separately and never rolled into one number.

---

### The five metrics

**1. Reach.** Percent of the roster with at least one documented encounter during the semester. Athletes with one or more encounters, divided by the official roster count. This separates a trainer who touches the whole roster from one who sees five athletes constantly. Both can be appropriate. They are different patterns, and we should know which one we have.

**2. Volume.** Treatment and rehab sessions per rostered athlete, per season, with fall and spring reported separately. Total documented sessions divided by roster count. Raw session counts mean nothing across sports with different roster sizes; per-athlete is the only fair comparison. For scale, one current staff member averaged 29 treatments per athlete in the fall and 44 in the spring.

**3. Time.** Estimated hours of direct athlete care: total sessions multiplied by 30 minutes. The 30 minutes is an assumption and every report will label it as one. We are using it because session durations are not consistently documented yet. Once they are, recorded times replace the assumption.

**4. Complexity.** Two numbers: distinct modality types used per athlete, and the percent of sessions involving advanced modalities (shockwave, dry needling, cupping). A hundred ice bags and a hundred dry needling sessions are not the same workload or the same skill. Session counts alone hide that.

**5. Availability impact.** Percent of athlete-days lost to injury and the time-loss injury rate, pulled from Kitman availability records. This is the number coaches and leadership actually care about: who is on the field. It is also the metric most exposed to documentation gaps, which is why data quality gets graded first.

---

### How we know whose data we can compare

Record keeping varies across teams. We are not going to judge anyone's documentation by opinion. Each team's data gets a completeness grade (A, B, or C) based on four things visible in the records themselves:

1. Does reach make sense next to the injury log? Injuries with no treatment sessions attached mean care happened off the books.
2. Are there in-season weeks with zero entries for a roster in active competition?
3. What percent of sessions have modality, body part, and duration filled in?
4. Do availability changes have a matching injury record, and the other way around?

Benchmark thresholds come from A and B teams only. C teams still appear in every report; their numbers carry a note that documentation limits comparison. Nobody is excluded, and nobody is graded against a standard their data cannot support.

---

### How the benchmarks get set

Teams are grouped with peers first, by contact level and roster size. Then thresholds are cut at terciles within each group: lower third, middle third, upper third of the observed range.

Year one is descriptive, not evaluative. The first annual report establishes what normal looks like here. It does not rank or penalize anyone. Evaluation against benchmarks starts only after the documentation standards have been in place for a full cycle.

---

### What this asks of staff

The minimum documentation expectations document will spell out the specifics. The short version: every evaluation, treatment, and rehab session goes in Kitman with modality, body part, and duration; availability stays current; an injury record gets entered when an athlete is made inactive. The five metrics are only as good as those entries.

Push back on any of this now. The point is to settle the definitions once so we stop relitigating them every reporting cycle.
