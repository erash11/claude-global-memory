# Athletic Training Utilization Metrics
## Standard Definitions for Internal Benchmarking

**Draft v1 — June 2026 — For review and approval**

---

### Purpose

Right now "utilization" means different things to different people. Until we count the same things the same way, no comparison between teams is fair and no report to leadership is defensible. This page defines the five metrics we will use in all utilization reporting, the assumptions behind them, and how we will judge whether a team's data is complete enough to compare.

These definitions feed two things: the annual per-team utilization reports, and the minimum documentation expectations for staff.

---

### What Utilization Is (and Is Not)

**Utilization is active athlete care: documented encounters where a trainer evaluates, treats, or rehabs an athlete.**

Utilization is NOT availability. Being onsite, at practice, or on call is part of the job and matters, but it is presence, not utilization. Availability and utilization will be reported separately and never combined into one number.

---

### The Five Metrics

**1. Reach** — *How much of the roster are we touching?*
Percent of rostered athletes with at least one documented encounter during the semester.
Formula: athletes with ≥1 encounter ÷ official roster count.
Why it matters: separates "sees the whole roster" from "sees five athletes constantly." Both can be appropriate; they are different patterns and we should know which one we have.

**2. Volume** — *How much care is being delivered?*
Treatment and rehab sessions per rostered athlete, per season (fall and spring reported separately).
Formula: total documented sessions ÷ official roster count.
Why it matters: raw session counts are meaningless across sports with different roster sizes. Per-athlete normalization is the only fair comparison. Reference point: one current staff member averaged 29 treatments per athlete in fall and 44 in spring.

**3. Time** — *How many care hours does that represent?*
Estimated hours of direct athlete care.
Formula: total sessions × 30 minutes.
Stated assumption: until session durations are consistently documented, we assume 30 minutes per session. This is an estimate and will be labeled as one in every report. As duration documentation improves, actual recorded times replace the assumption.

**4. Complexity** — *What kind of care is being delivered?*
Two numbers: (a) distinct modality types used per athlete, and (b) percent of sessions involving advanced modalities (shockwave, dry needling, cupping, and similar).
Why it matters: a hundred ice bags and a hundred dry needling sessions are not the same workload or the same skill demand. This keeps the quality-of-care side of utilization visible instead of letting session counts tell the whole story.

**5. Availability Impact** — *What is the athlete health outcome?*
Percent of athlete-days lost to injury and the time-loss injury rate, from Kitman availability records.
Why it matters: connects training room work to what coaches and leadership actually care about, athletes on the field. This is also the metric most sensitive to documentation gaps, which is why data quality is scored first (below).

---

### Data Quality: How We Know What We Can Compare

Record keeping varies across teams. Rather than judging anyone's documentation by opinion, each team's data receives a completeness grade (A/B/C) based on four signals visible in the records themselves:

1. **Coverage plausibility** — does reach make sense next to logged injuries? Injuries with no associated treatment sessions indicate undocumented care.
2. **Cadence gaps** — in-season weeks with zero entries for a roster in active competition.
3. **Field completeness** — percent of sessions with modality, body part, and duration actually filled in.
4. **Internal consistency** — availability status changes that have a matching injury record, and vice versa.

Benchmark thresholds are calculated from A and B teams only. C teams still appear in every report, with their numbers flagged as "documentation completeness limits comparison." No one is excluded; no one is graded against a standard their data cannot support.

---

### How Benchmarks Will Be Set

- Teams are grouped with peers (contact level, roster size) before any comparison.
- Within each peer group, thresholds are set at terciles: lower third, middle third, upper third of the observed range.
- **Year one is descriptive, not evaluative.** The first annual report establishes what normal looks like at Baylor. It does not rank, reward, or penalize anyone. Evaluation against benchmarks begins only after documentation standards have been in place for a full cycle.

---

### What This Asks of Staff

The companion minimum documentation expectations document will spell out the specifics, but the short version: every evaluation, treatment, and rehab session documented in Kitman with modality, body part, and duration; availability kept current; injury records entered when athletes are made inactive. The five metrics above are only as good as those entries.
