# Reading the post-practice report

**For:** Baylor Volleyball coaching staff
**Time needed:** five minutes, once.

After each practice you get one page. This explains what is on it, what it means,
and — just as importantly — what it cannot tell you.

---

## The short version

The Kinexon sensors count what every athlete actually did in practice: how many
jumps, how hard, how densely packed into the session. The report turns that into
three things:

1. **What kind of session that was** — one sentence at the top.
2. **Who is worth a second look** — often one or two names, sometimes nobody.
3. **Everyone else, in order** — so you can see the whole squad at a glance.

Everything else on the page supports those three.

## Why the totals can lie to you, and what we use instead

Practice 6 of the camp block was **second-lowest of the six on jump total** — 900
against a block average of 1,015. It was also the **second-densest session of the
block**: 9.4 jumps a minute against a block average of 6.8, because it ran 1h 35m
when some of the others ran over three hours.

Read the total on its own and Practice 6 looks like a light day. It wasn't.

So the top line always gives you both — the total and the rate — and says where
the session sat against the rest of the block:

> *Second-shortest session of the block. 900 jumps at 9.39/min, against a block
> average of 1,015 and 6.78/min.*

That sentence is generated from the numbers, not written by anyone. It states
where the session sat and stops there. It will never tell you a session was
"too hard" or that you "should" do anything differently — that judgement is
yours, and the data isn't strong enough to make it for you.

When a session is unremarkable, it says so: *"A typical session for this block on
volume and density."*

## The one idea that changes how you read the page

**Above her own normal is coloured as risk, not as progress.**

This is the thing most worth holding onto, and it is the opposite of how these
charts usually read.

Everything the sensors measure is *work absorbed* — how much, how hard, how
densely. A high number does not mean an athlete performed well. It means she took
on more than she usually does. That is a demand on her, so it gets the warm
colour. Below her normal gets the cool colour: worth noticing, but not the thing
that hurts anyone.

The one place this flips is force plate testing. There, the number is
*performance* rather than work absorbed, so a **drop** is the direction that
matters. If you see that on a page, it's flipped deliberately.

## "Compared to what?" — always her, never the squad

Every comparison on the page is **an athlete against her own average**, across the
practices in that block.

Not against the squad. A libero and a middle blocker do not do the same job, and
comparing their jump counts tells you nothing you didn't already know. Not against
a league or national benchmark either — none exists for volleyball, and inventing
one would be worse than saying so.

## What "worth a look" actually means

An athlete appears in that section when her number for the day sits further from
her own normal **than she usually swings**. Not further than the squad — further
than *she* typically is, session to session.

Two honest limits on it:

- **It is a prompt, not a finding.** With six practices behind each average, that
  average is thin. The section is telling you where to point your eyes, not what
  happened. The page prints the number of practices it used so you can weigh it
  yourself.
- **The count moves with the session, which is how you know it's working.** Across
  the six practices of the camp block it named 1, 3, 7, 0, 0 and 1 athlete. The
  seven came on Practice 3 — by a distance the heaviest day of the block — and two
  quiet days named nobody at all. If it flagged the same handful every session
  regardless of what happened, it would be measuring the athletes rather than the
  training, and we'd want to know.

If an athlete has been with the squad for fewer practices than everyone else, her
average rests on less, and the page says so next to her name rather than quietly
treating it as solid.

## Why a name might be greyed out

Near the top there is a line like *"15 of 16 athletes with usable data. 1 excluded
— see notes."*

Sometimes a sensor slips, an athlete is tagged to the wrong position, or someone
leaves early. When that happens the number is real in the data and meaningless in
practice — one athlete in the camp block recorded 23 jumps across three days
against a group median of 379.

Rather than show her at "−74% against her own normal" as though that were a
training story, the report does two things:

- **Leaves her out of "worth a look"**, so a sensor fault never occupies a slot
  meant for an athlete who needs your attention.
- **Keeps her bar in the list below, greyed, with the reason written underneath.**
  Nothing is deleted. That full list doubles as your attendance and equipment
  check, and hiding a problem from it would defeat the point.

An athlete is only excluded once one of us has actually looked and decided. Until
then the report flags the oddity as **unreviewed** and still counts her
everywhere. It will never quietly drop someone on its own.

## What the report will not tell you

Stated plainly, because a tool that hides its limits is worse than one that has
them:

- **Whether a block left anyone fatigued.** That needs a force plate test at each
  end of a block, and right now those don't exist. The comparison is built and
  waiting; it fills in the day the tests are recorded.
- **Whether load is trending up or down over weeks.** The standard measure for
  that needs roughly four weeks of history. This block is three days. It appears
  on its own once enough practices have been collected — nothing needs building.
- **Which part of a practice was heavy.** For five of the six practices we can
  tell you the session was demanding, but not *which drill made it so*. See below.
- **Anything heart-rate based.** Five heart-rate columns come through in the
  export and every value in all of them is zero. That is not something a
  different practice setup would fix — we do not currently have heart-rate
  collection capability, so those columns stay empty until we do. Internal load,
  HR zones and recoveries are off the table for now; everything on this page is
  external load, which is what the sensors actually measure.

## Two things that would sharpen it, both on your side

Neither is a software problem. Both are collection.

### 1. Cutting sessions into drills in KINEXON OS

Practice 1 was exported drill by drill. Inside that one session:

| Drill | Length | Jumps per minute |
|---|---|---:|
| Box Drill | 7m | **26.8** |
| Hurricane | 10m | 12.4 |
| Correction Exchange | 20m | 11.4 |
| Bunch Drill | 35m | 6.6 |
| Whirlwind | 35m | 3.9 |
| Dynamic Warm Up | 12m | 1.9 |

Box Drill ran at **seven times the jump density of Whirlwind**, and fourteen times
the warm-up — inside a single practice. The session total for that day was 946
jumps at 5.0 a minute, which hides every bit of it.

The other five practices came across as a single block, so for those the report
can say the day was heavy but never which twenty minutes made it heavy.

If drill-level export became routine, the report stops describing what happened
and starts describing **what a practice plan costs** — which drills are expensive,
and what swapping one for another actually buys you. That is a different and far
more useful thing to have in front of you when you're planning.

**The question back to you:** what does it take to cut a session into drills in
KINEXON OS, and is that sustainable in-season? If it's ten minutes of tagging,
it is comfortably worth it.

### 2. A jump test at each end of a camp block

Two force plate tests per block, about five minutes per athlete, no change to how
practice runs. That is what turns "here is what they absorbed" into "here is what
it cost them, and who it cost most."

The full case is a separate page — `docs/volleyball-jump-testing-ask.md` — argued
from this block's own numbers.
